# Submission Abstract

Modern retrieval and recommendation systems are increasingly organized around shared latent representations rather than explicit rule-based matching. This project investigates whether such systems can be understood as learned information landscapes: structured geometries in which semantically adjacent states cluster, popularity bias shapes the visible surface, and hidden frontier concepts remain recoverable through basin-like associative dynamics. The thesis is grounded in three linked frameworks: two-tower retrieval architectures, contrastive learning via InfoNCE, and modern Hopfield networks. Together, these approaches suggest that retrieval is geometric and associative rather than purely lexical, and that semantically adjacent probes can reveal structure that is not visible from direct keyword matching alone.

This project operationalizes the claim in a bounded synthetic setting. A hidden frontier of concepts is embedded in a niche research field, while a popularity-biased recommender exposes a noisy and incomplete neighborhood around that structure. We evaluate whether a heterogeneous ensemble of synthetic personas, each seeded with adjacent but non-explicit technical terms, can recover more of the frontier than a lexical baseline. The simulator demonstrates that this is possible under controlled conditions: ensemble probing recovers a stronger signal than direct lexical matching while remaining within a synthetic and aggregate research frame.

The contribution is not a live-platform deployment claim or a proposal for individualized targeting. Rather, it is a defensible research program for studying how latent-field geometry shapes retrieval and recommendation. By connecting the PDF thesis to supporting literature in representation learning and associative memory, the project offers a coherent framework for testing how semantic neighborhoods, attractor basins, and frontier reconstruction interact in modern information systems.
